Critical concepts to master

Within-person vs between-person

The entire project operates at the within-person level. When THIS person sleeps worse than usual, does their mood deteriorate? This is fundamentally different from comparing people who sleep less vs more.

Why RI-CLPM, not CLPM

The classic cross-lagged panel model confounds stable trait differences with dynamic within-person change. The random-intercept version (Hamaker et al., 2015) separates these — making the cross-lagged effects genuinely within-person.

IS, IV, L5, M10, RA

The five circadian metrics from actigraphy you must be able to explain from memory. Each captures a different aspect of rhythm quality. All are typically disrupted in bipolar disorder.

Sleep as both symptom and trigger

In bipolar disorder, sleep loss is a symptom of mania — but it is also a causal trigger that can precipitate manic episodes. This bidirectionality is exactly what the project is designed to quantify.

The Project
Understanding "The Night and Day of Bipolar Disorder"
Core research question: How do circadian rhythm disturbances relate to lifestyle factors, mood variation, and risk of new illness episodes in people who are early in the course of bipolar disorder?

Study Design

This is an intensive longitudinal design (ILD) — a design where measurements are repeated many times within individuals, over a long period. Unlike typical cross-sectional or even standard longitudinal studies, ILD captures the within-person dynamics that standard designs miss entirely.

What ILD captures

  • Day-to-day fluctuations in sleep and mood
  • Temporal ordering: what comes before what
  • Within-person patterns that differ from group averages
  • Person-specific idiographic dynamics

What standard designs miss

  • Two people can have the same average sleep — but one is stable, one is wildly variable
  • Averages across people obscure individual-level dynamics
  • Between-person effects do not generalize to within-person predictions

Data Sources

Actigraphy

Wrist-worn accelerometer worn continuously. Provides objective, continuous measurement of movement — from which sleep/wake patterns and rest-activity rhythms are derived. Days to months of data per participant.

App-based EMA

Ecological Momentary Assessment via smartphone app. Multiple prompts per day capturing mood, energy, sleep quality, daily activities. Subjective but ecologically valid — captures experience in real time.

Clinical Context

Patients are recruited through Bipolarteamet at Nydalen DPS — the outpatient bipolar disorder team at Nydalen District Psychiatric Center. This is a clinical research environment: patients are active patients, clinicians are busy, and data collection happens in the real world, not a controlled lab.

Why "early course"? Studying patients early in the course of BD — before the accumulation of episodes, before significant neuroprogression, before lifestyle and medication effects become deeply confounded — gives the best window on the mechanisms themselves. Intervention at this stage may also prevent future episodes.

What the project aims to establish

Question 1
Description: What do rest-activity rhythms and sleep look like in early-course BD? How do they differ across mood states?
Question 2
Direction: Does circadian disruption precede mood deterioration, or does mood deterioration cause circadian disruption — or both? This requires intensive longitudinal methods that can detect temporal ordering.
Question 3
Prediction / translation: Can circadian features predict new mood episodes? Are there individual circadian signatures that mark higher risk?

Actigraphy & Circadian Methods
From raw acceleration to circadian phenotypes

What actigraphy is and what it measures

Actigraphy uses a small wrist-worn accelerometer to continuously record movement. The device does not directly measure sleep — it measures acceleration. Sleep/wake classifications are derived by applying validated algorithms to the acceleration signal.

Key distinction: Actigraphy infers sleep from the absence of movement and related features. It cannot measure sleep stages (NREM1, NREM2, slow-wave, REM). For that you need polysomnography (PSG) — which is impractical for weeks-long naturalistic studies. Actigraphy trades accuracy for ecological validity and duration.

From raw data to sleep variables

Raw data is sampled at 25–100 Hz (device-dependent). This is typically averaged into epochs (e.g., 15-second or 1-minute windows). Sleep/wake classification algorithms (Cole-Kripke, Sadeh, or GGIR's own) then label each epoch.

Rest-Activity Rhythm (RAR) Metrics

These capture the circadian structure of the activity pattern across the full 24-hour cycle — not just the sleep window. They require longer observation windows (typically 7–14+ days). All are computable from actigraphy with the GGIR package or similar tools.

MetricWhat it capturesTypical finding in BD
IS — Interdaily StabilityCorrelation between each day's activity profile and the mean 24-hr profile. HIGH IS = stable, consistent rhythm across days. Range 0–1.Reduced — day-to-day rhythms are less consistent
IV — Intradaily VariabilityFragmentation of the rhythm within a day (transitions between active and rest). HIGH IV = choppy, fragmented rhythm. Range 0–3+.Elevated — more fragmented within-day pattern
L5Least active 5-hour window — timing (L5-midpoint) reflects circadian phase; intensity reflects depth of restL5 timing shifted; L5 intensity often elevated (less complete rest)
M10Most active 10-hour window — timing reflects peak activity phase; intensity reflects activity levelM10 timing shifted; M10 intensity often reduced
RA — Relative Amplitude(M10 − L5) / (M10 + L5). Range 0–1. HIGH RA = strong contrast between active and rest periods = robust rhythmReduced — less contrast between day and night activity
IS and IV are independent: A person can have high IS (same rhythm every day) but high IV (that rhythm is fragmented within the day). Always interpret them separately. The combination matters: low IS + high IV = maximally disrupted rhythm.

The Two-Process Model of Sleep Regulation

Process S — Homeostatic

Sleep pressure builds during wakefulness (driven by adenosine accumulation). It dissipates during sleep. The longer you stay awake, the greater the pressure to sleep. This explains why sleep deprivation leads to rebound sleep.

Process C — Circadian

The biological clock (~24 hour oscillator, driven by the suprachiasmatic nucleus). It gates sleep independently of how long you've been awake — promoting wakefulness during the day and sleep at night. Driven by light exposure (the primary zeitgeber).

Sleep onset occurs when both S (high sleep pressure) and C (clock promoting sleep) align. In bipolar disorder, both processes are dysregulated: sleep pressure recovery may be abnormal, and the circadian oscillator shows phase and amplitude irregularities.

The GGIR Package in R

GGIR is the standard open-source R package for processing raw accelerometry data from research-grade devices. Key functions:

  • Non-wear detection: identifies periods when the device is not worn (sustained very low acceleration; confirmed by flat temperature signal on devices with temperature sensors)
  • Calibration correction: corrects for device-level calibration drift
  • Sleep detection: applies validated algorithms (HDCZ or HSPT) to identify sleep windows
  • RAR calculation: computes IS, IV, L5, M10, RA automatically
  • Output: person-level and day-level summary files ready for multilevel analysis

Actigraphy vs Polysomnography

Actigraphy strengths

  • Weeks to months of continuous data
  • Naturalistic — participant sleeps at home
  • Low burden for participants
  • Captures circadian rhythms across full day
  • Feasible in psychiatric outpatient populations

Actigraphy limitations

  • Cannot detect sleep stages (NREM/REM)
  • Tends to misclassify quiet wakefulness as sleep
  • Overestimates sleep duration, underestimates WASO
  • Participant compliance required (must actually wear device)
  • No EEG signal — misses micro-arousals

What actigraphy literature shows in bipolar disorder

  • Reduced IS and elevated IV — consistently replicated across studies
  • Reduced RA — less robust contrast between active and rest periods
  • Differences between mood states: mania shows reduced sleep and altered phase; depression shows hypersomnia or sleep fragmentation depending on episode subtype
  • Euthymic BD patients show intermediate values between healthy controls and episode states — circadian disruption is not only an episode phenomenon
  • Within-person variability is high — between-episode data can look near-normal for some patients; others remain chronically disrupted

App-based EMA — key concepts

Signal-contingent

Prompts sent at random times. Best for capturing representative snapshots. Reduces recall bias.

Event-contingent

Participant initiates when an event occurs (e.g., a mood shift). Captures important but rare events.

Interval-contingent

Fixed-time prompts (e.g., morning and evening). Lower frequency but predictable — reduces burden.

Burden-compliance tradeoff: More prompts per day = richer data but lower compliance. In psychiatric outpatient populations, 2–4 prompts/day is typically feasible without unacceptable dropout. The optimal protocol balances data richness with real-world feasibility.
Merging EMA and actigraphy: Both generate time-stamped data streams at different sampling frequencies. Merging requires careful timestamp alignment, handling of time zones, and decisions about how to aggregate (e.g., average actigraphy over the hour preceding an EMA prompt). Missing data patterns differ between streams.
Statistical Methods
Intensive longitudinal data, cross-lagged models, and causal direction
The fundamental distinction: Between-person effects tell you about differences across people. Within-person effects tell you about fluctuations within one person over time. This project is about within-person dynamics. Most of the statistical methods here exist specifically to isolate within-person effects.

Why this distinction matters so much

Imagine two findings:

  • Between-person: People who sleep fewer hours on average tend to report worse mood on average.
  • Within-person: When a person sleeps fewer hours than their own average on a given night, they report worse mood the next day.

These are different questions with different answers. The between-person finding might just reflect that people with depression sleep less AND have worse mood — both caused by depression, not sleep causing mood. The within-person finding is more causally meaningful. The project requires methods that answer the within-person question.

Classic Cross-Lagged Panel Model (CLPM)

The CLPM models the relationship between two variables across measurement occasions, estimating how X at time t predicts Y at time t+1 (cross-lagged path), while controlling for Y at time t.

The fatal flaw: The CLPM does not distinguish between stable between-person differences and dynamic within-person fluctuations. If person A consistently scores high on both X and Y (because of a trait), this will appear as a cross-lagged effect — even if within-person, X and Y are unrelated. The CLPM treats stable individual differences as if they were dynamic processes.

Random-Intercept CLPM (RI-CLPM)

Introduced by Hamaker, Kuiper & Grasman (2015) in Psychological Methods. The solution: add person-level random intercepts that absorb the stable between-person variance. The cross-lagged paths then operate only on the within-person residuals — the fluctuations around each person's own mean.

What RI-CLPM recovers

  • Cross-lagged effects that are truly within-person
  • Clean separation of trait (random intercept) from state (within-person residual)
  • More conservative estimates — often smaller than CLPM
  • Conceptually aligned with what the project cares about

Limitations of RI-CLPM

  • Assumes stationarity (same dynamics at every time point)
  • Fixed lag — must choose a lag length (daily? 2-day?)
  • Assumes all persons have the same cross-lagged dynamics (homogeneous)
  • Requires a reasonable number of measurement occasions per person

Dynamic Structural Equation Modeling (DSEM)

DSEM (Asparouhov, Hamaker & Muthen) is the state-of-the-art framework for intensive longitudinal data. It extends multilevel time-series analysis into the SEM framework:

  • Handles contemporaneous (same-time) and lagged relations
  • Models both between-person and within-person levels simultaneously
  • Accommodates person-level random effects on dynamic parameters (e.g., some people may have stronger cross-lagged effects than others)
  • Can handle irregular measurement intervals
  • Implemented in Mplus (via MplusAutomation package in R)
DSEM vs RI-CLPM: DSEM is more flexible but also more complex. RI-CLPM is implemented in structural equation modeling software (lavaan in R) and is more accessible. For this project, either could work depending on data structure and research question specifics. Be prepared to discuss both.

Vector Autoregression (VAR)

VAR models a system of time series where each variable is regressed on its own past values and the past values of all other variables. In a bivariate VAR(1):

  • X(t) = a1·X(t-1) + b1·Y(t-1) + error
  • Y(t) = a2·X(t-1) + b2·Y(t-1) + error

The off-diagonal elements (b1 and b2) are the cross-lagged effects — equivalent to Granger causality tests.

Granger causality

X Granger-causes Y if past values of X significantly improve prediction of Y beyond Y's own history alone. This is predictive precedence, not true causation. It is sensitive to omitted variables and the chosen lag length.

Idiographic VAR (N=1)

Fit a separate VAR model for each individual. Captures person-specific dynamics — the coupling between circadian disruption and mood may differ substantially across people. The gimme package in R groups people by similar patterns.

Multilevel Models for ILD

When data are repeated observations nested within persons, ordinary regression violates the independence assumption. Multilevel models (also called mixed-effects models or hierarchical linear models) account for this nesting explicitly:

  • Level 1: Measurement occasion (the observation) — within-person effects
  • Level 2: Person — between-person effects
  • Random effects allow regression coefficients to vary across persons
  • Implemented in R via lme4 (frequentist) or brms (Bayesian)

Choosing a lag length

All lagged models require a choice of lag (how far back to look). This is both a statistical and a substantive question:

  • Theory-driven: If the biological mechanism takes ~24 hours to operate, a 1-day lag makes sense. If you expect near-immediate effects, intraday lags may be needed.
  • Data-driven: Fit models with different lags; use information criteria (AIC/BIC) to compare. Check autocorrelation functions (ACFs) for natural lag structure in the data.
  • Practical constraint: Daily lag aligns with EMA assessment schedule (morning/evening prompts) and makes biological sense for sleep-mood cycles.

Bipolar Disorder & Circadian Biology
Clinical foundations and the science of sleep-mood relationships

Bipolar Disorder — Diagnostic Foundations

Bipolar I (BD-I)

Requires at least one full manic episode (7+ days, or less if requiring hospitalization). Depressive episodes common but not required for diagnosis. Manic episodes may include psychotic features.

Bipolar II (BD-II)

Requires at least one hypomanic episode (4+ days, not severe enough for hospitalization) AND at least one major depressive episode. No full manic episodes. BD-II is not a milder form — depressive burden is often greater.

Mood episode types

EpisodeCore featuresSleep pattern
ManiaElevated/irritable mood, grandiosity, decreased need for sleep, racing thoughts, impulsivity, psychosis possibleMarkedly reduced sleep without daytime fatigue — patient feels rested on 2–3 hours
HypomaniaSame as mania but less severe, shorter, no psychosis, no significant functional impairmentReduced sleep; energized
DepressionDepressed mood, anhedonia, psychomotor changes, cognitive impairmentInsomnia OR hypersomnia; fatigue; poor sleep quality
Mixed statesSimultaneous manic and depressive features — highest suicide riskHighly disrupted; often insomnia
EuthymiaBetween episodes — clinically stable, but residual circadian disruption commonIntermediate — closer to normal but often not fully normalized

Sleep Disturbance: Symptom, Trigger, or Both?

This is one of the most important conceptual questions for the interview. The relationship between sleep and bipolar disorder is bidirectional:

Sleep disruption as symptom

Reduced sleep need is a core feature of mania (DSM-5 criterion). Insomnia and hypersomnia are common in depressive episodes. In this direction, mood state causes sleep changes.

Sleep disruption as trigger

Sleep deprivation can precipitate manic episodes in vulnerable individuals. Even one night of poor sleep can shift mood upward. Social rhythm disruption (travel, shift work, social events) can destabilize mood. In this direction, sleep change causes mood change.

Why this matters for the project: The project directly tries to quantify this bidirectional relationship using RI-CLPM or DSEM — does circadian disruption at time T predict mood at T+1 (sleep → mood), or does mood at T predict sleep at T+1 (mood → sleep), or both? This is the core scientific contribution.

Circadian Biology in Bipolar Disorder

What is known

  • Phase abnormalities: Delayed sleep phase is common, especially in depression. Some patients show advanced phase in mania.
  • Rhythm irregularity: Day-to-day variability in sleep timing is elevated in BD, even in euthymia (captured by low IS).
  • Melatonin: Supersensitivity to light-induced melatonin suppression in BD — the circadian system is more reactive to light cues.
  • Cortisol: Altered diurnal cortisol patterns, particularly HPA axis dysregulation in depression.
  • Social rhythms: Disruption of regular daily routines (meals, sleep, social contact) destabilizes the circadian system — a key mechanism in the social rhythm theory of BD.

Social Rhythm Therapy (SRT)

SRT is a behavioral intervention developed specifically for bipolar disorder. Its core premise is that regularity of social rhythms entrains the circadian clock. Social routines (waking time, first contact with others, meals, bedtime) act as social zeitgebers (time-givers) that synchronize the biological clock.

  • Developed by Ellen Frank and colleagues at Pittsburgh
  • Often combined with Interpersonal therapy as IPSRT (Interpersonal and Social Rhythm Therapy)
  • Targets: stable sleep-wake times, regular meal times, stable daily activity patterns
  • Evidence: reduces episode recurrence, particularly manic episodes
  • Research connection: If the project shows that circadian irregularity (low IS, high IV) temporally precedes mood shifts, this directly supports the SRT mechanism and strengthens the case for regularity-based interventions

Why Early-Course Matters

Neuroprogression: Recurrent mood episodes are associated with cognitive decline, structural brain changes, and increasing episode frequency (kindling effect). Studying patients early in the illness course captures the mechanisms before they are confounded by chronicity.

Intervention window: Early identification of circadian markers that predict episode onset could enable targeted prevention — circadian-based interventions (chronotherapy, light therapy, SRT) before an episode establishes itself.

Etiology vs consequence: In chronic patients, it becomes hard to separate illness mechanisms from medication effects, lifestyle adaptation, and secondary consequences. Early-course patients give a cleaner window.

BD in the Context of a Psychosis Research Section

The OUS section is Seksjon for klinisk psykoseforskning. BD is studied here because:

  • BD-I with psychotic features overlaps diagnostically with schizoaffective disorder
  • Dimensional approaches see psychosis and mood disorder on a continuum
  • Circadian disruption is also prominent in schizophrenia — findings may generalize
  • NORMENT (the Norwegian Centre for Mental Disorders Research) at OUS spans both psychosis and BD
The Three-Paper PhD Plan
A coherent arc from description to mechanism to clinical translation
Norwegian PhDs typically consist of three peer-reviewed articles plus a comprehensive introduction (kappe/sammenstilling). The three papers should form a logical arc: each building on the previous, together addressing the project's core questions.

Paper 1 — Characterizing circadian and sleep phenotypes across mood states

Research question

How do actigraphy-derived sleep and rest-activity rhythm variables differ across mood states (euthymia, depression, hypomania) in patients with early-course bipolar disorder?

Design and methods

Observational. Use the longitudinal dataset, classifying observation windows by mood state (EMA or clinical assessment). Compare IS, IV, L5/M10 timing and intensity, RA, WASO, SE, and TST across mood states using linear mixed-effects models (lme4 in R) accounting for the nested structure.

Scientific contribution

Establishes the circadian signature of mood states in this specific population. Replicates and extends prior actigraphy literature in an early-course, intensively measured sample. Identifies which metrics are most sensitive to mood state — informing the variable selection for Papers 2 and 3.

Paper 2 — Temporal dynamics: does circadian disruption precede or follow mood changes?

Research question

What are the bidirectional temporal relationships between objective circadian rhythm parameters (actigraphy) and self-reported mood and energy (EMA) within individuals in early-course bipolar disorder?

Design and methods

Intensive longitudinal. Use RI-CLPM (Hamaker et al., 2015) or DSEM to model the cross-lagged relationship between daily actigraphy variables (e.g., SE, WASO, IS computed over rolling windows) and daily EMA mood/energy ratings. The critical methodological choice: random intercepts to isolate within-person dynamics. Lag choice informed by theory (24-hour cycles) and data (ACF inspection).

Scientific contribution

This is the flagship paper — directly addressing the causal direction question at the heart of the project. Distinguishes whether circadian disruption is a prodromal marker of mood deterioration or a consequence. Provides evidence for or against the chronobiological model of BD.

Key methodological challenges to anticipate

  • Sufficient measurement occasions per person for stable within-person estimates
  • Irregular measurement intervals (device non-wear, app non-response)
  • Choice of actigraphy variable (daily vs rolling window)
  • Whether to use person-average or standardized within-person variables

Paper 3 — Individual circadian signatures as predictors of new mood episodes

Research question

Do person-specific temporal coupling patterns between circadian rhythms and mood predict the occurrence of new mood episodes in early-course BD? Are there clinically distinguishable circadian phenotype subgroups?

Design and methods — two possible approaches

Option A (Idiographic prediction): Fit person-level (N=1) VAR models or use GIMME to extract individual coupling parameters. Use these parameters as predictors of time-to-next-episode in survival analysis. Tests whether the strength of circadian-mood coupling predicts episode risk.

Option B (Phenotype subgroups): Latent class or cluster analysis of circadian rhythm profiles (IS, IV, RA, phase). Characterize clinical and outcome differences between circadian phenotype classes. More accessible for clinical translation.

Scientific contribution

Translates group-level findings into clinically meaningful individual risk markers. Supports the feasibility of actigraphy-based early warning systems. Connects basic circadian science to clinical prediction.

Prosjektbeskrivelse

Prosjekttittel: "The night and day of bipolar disorder"

Personer med bipolar lidelse har ofte søvnvansker og utfordringer med døgnrytmeregulering. Men kan dårlig eller uregelmessig søvn utløse nye depressive eller maniske episoder? Eller er døgnrytme-forstyrrelser heller et symptom på en stemningsepisode?

Stipendiaten i dette prosjektet skal bidra til å undersøke hvordan forstyrrelser i døgnrytme henger sammen med livsstilsfaktorer, variasjoner i stemningsleie og risiko for nye sykdomsepisoder hos personer tidlig i forløpet av bipolar lidelse. Med intensive longitudinelle data fra aktigrafi og app-basert selvrapportering, skal det benyttes statistiske metoder egnet for å undersøke retning og styrke i ulike sammenhenger.

Prosjektet har klinisk forankring i Bipolarteamet ved Nydalen DPS, og forskningskoordinerende evt. klinisk forskningsarbeid i teamet vil inngå i stillingen. Prosjektet vil utføres i samarbeid med statistiker og andre veiledere, og være tilknyttet forskningsgruppen «Kliniske symptommekanismer», ledet av professor Trine Vik Lagerberg. Gruppen fokuserer bl.a. på psykologiske mekanismer ved bipolar lidelse og utvikling og bruk av digitale verktøy for klinisk karakterisering og behandling.

Arbeidsoppgaver

  • Behandle og kvalitetssikre aktigrafi- og kliniske data
  • Analysere data i samarbeid med statistikere
  • Gjennomfare praktisk forskningsarbeid inkludert kliniske intervjuer og datainnsamling
  • Publisere funn i internasjonale tidsskrifter

Allmennpsykiatrisk poliklinikk, Nydalen DPS

Pasientrekruttering til prosjektet skjer gjennom Bipolarteamet ved Nydalen DPS. Nydalen DPS er et distriktspsykiatrisk senter som blant annet driver Allmennpsykiatrisk poliklinikk.

Adresse: Gullhaugveien 12, 0484 Oslo
Telefon: 23 02 33 00 (mandag–fredag 08.00–15.30)
Opptaksomrade: Bydelene Sagene, Bjerke og Nordre Aker

Om poliklinikken

Poliklinikken tilbyr utredning og behandling til voksne over 18 ar med moderate til alvorlige psykiske lidelser som krever spesialisthelsetjeneste. Typisk behandlingsforlop er 10–15 sesjoner a 45 minutter, med individuell terapeut.

Tjenester

  • Individuell terapi
  • Gruppekurs: sosial angst, traumastabilisering, psykoedukasjon ved bipolar lidelse, mentaliseringsbasert terapi ved personlighetsforstyrrelser
  • Medikamentell behandling ved alvorlig angst, depresjon eller psykose
  • Helhetlig oppfolging inkludert fysisk helse, livsstil og sovn

Tilgang

  • Henvisning fra fastlege eller psykolog
  • Innledende vurdering avgjor om pasienten oppfyller kravene
  • Ventetid for poliklinisk vurdering: ca. 8 uker

Bipolar lidelse — behandling ved OUS

Informasjon fra Oslo universitetssykehus om behandling av bipolar lidelse:

Medikamentell behandling

  • Litium: hyppig brukt, krever naye dosering og blodprovetaking
  • Antipsykotika: brukes ved maniske faser
  • Stemningstabiliserende midler: ofte ved depressive episoder

Psykologiske tilnaerminger

  • Psykoedukasjon om lidelsen og mestring
  • Interpersonlig sosial rytmeterapi (IPSRT)
  • Kognitiv atferdsterapi (KAT)
  • Familiebasert behandling
Klinisk relevans for prosjektet: Behandlingstilbudet ved Nydalen DPS inkluderer eksplisitt sovnintervensjoner og cirkadianstabilisering som del av bipolaromsorgen. Prosjektet "The night and day of bipolar disorder" springer direkte ut av dette kliniske miljoet — og funn fra forskningen kan direkte informere og forbedre det eksisterende behandlingstilbudet.
